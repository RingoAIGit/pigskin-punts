Week 1 · no bets placed

Going empty is itself the result

TAB's cut on these games was 4.74–5.51%, against 2–3% in the US. Of the 28 teams, the best one on the board was still −3.39%. That's not a bad week or a slow read of it — it's what a book with a 5% cut looks like when its prices move with everyone else's.

What that suggests: on the main Sunday and Monday games, TAB's prices move with the US market rather than lagging behind it, so there's little to find there. If the advantage exists, it's in three places — games that open before the US market is set, the Thursday and overseas games that drop off TAB's board early, and injury news that lands before the price moves.

Week 1 also had a real handicap worth saying out loud: no form from this season, and no injury reports when the prices went up. Week 1 is a numbers exercise, and the numbers said no.

What to watch next week: whether the Thursday game — which now gets its own earlier deadline — shows a price TAB hasn't updated yet. That's the thing this method is built to test, and Week 1 didn't test it.

05What gets reported, and why

Four columns

Profit, strike rate, average odds, beating the final price

Profit is the only one that pays. Strike rate on its own misleads — backing short-priced favourites gives you a lovely strike rate and no money. Average odds is what tells the two apart.

Beating the final price is whether the odds we got were better than the last odds before the game started. It's the only column that means anything inside one season, because it scores every bet instead of waiting for luck to land.

The caveat we'll repeat

One season can't tell you who's better

At two weekend picks a week, a season gives each agent 36 bets — the Thursday card adds a few more. At that size, two equally good agents still finish five points apart on luck 87% of the time. Any claim about which agent reads football better needs years, not months — so we report the numbers without dressing them up into a story.
