The method

One question, asked honestly every week: is TAB's price better than the true price? Everything below is how we answer it.

01What we're actually trying to beat

TAB is the only place in New Zealand you can legally bet on sport — nobody else is allowed to take sports bets from people here. That has one big consequence: we can't shop around for a better price.

With only one book we can't dodge its cut by taking the best price elsewhere. That cut is a tax we just pay. On this week's games TAB's cut was 4.74% to 5.51%; the US books work on 2–3%.

So every bet at TAB starts about 2.5 points behind before we've even looked at the two teams. The one advantage we can reliably find is a price TAB hasn't got around to updating — one the US market has already moved past.

02The bar

We only back a team if TAB's price is 4.0% or better than the true US price — the US price with the bookies' cut taken out. Anything under 4%, and TAB's cut eats the whole advantage.

Step 1

Read TAB's own prices

We read TAB's prices straight from the feed behind its own website, at api.tab.co.nz. No login needed, and never a third-party "best odds" page — those are someone else's numbers, not TAB's.

Step 2

Get the true price from the US

The middle price from the big US books (DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM, Caesars, BetRivers, Fanatics and others), with their cut taken out. The middle one rather than the average, so one odd book can't drag it around.

Step 3

Compare, then decide

advantage = TAB price ÷ true US price − 1, plus the extra credit for the points line explained below. Under 4%, no bet — and saying so is a result.

03The advantage is in the points line, not the odds

On who-wins bets TAB is about 5% worse than the true price, so those will almost never clear the bar. The advantage has to come from the points line — if TAB has the game at +4 and the market has +3.5, that half point is real value even when the odds are identical.

What half a point is worth

These come from real games, 2011–2025, counting only games where the line sat within a point of the key number. A half point matters when it turns a draw into a result: the game lands exactly on the old number, so at one line you get your money back and at the other you win or lose. Counting games where either team wins by that margin roughly doubles every figure, which is wrong.

The rule that matters more than the table

The extra credit applies only when the half point sits across the key number — that is, the two lines are k−0.5 and k, or k and k+0.5. Moving from +3.5 to +4 does not cross 3; it swaps one kind of four-point loss for another, and it's worth the 4-value, about 2.6%. Half points at 5, 8, 9 and 11–13 are worth very little.

04The money

Said out loud, not buried

NZ$5 out of a NZ$62 pot is 8% a bet

Most people bet 1–3% of their pot at a time. A normal bad run of 6–8 losing bets — which happens to good systems too — would leave us about NZ$25–35.

Two ways out, either is fine: put more in (NZ$150–200 makes NZ$5 about 3% of the pot), or bet NZ$2 a time, about 3.2%, with no new money. Neither is decided — it's Neil's call, not the agents'.

05What this method can't do

Stated up front, so it doesn't get argued about later.

  • It can't make a bad book good. A 5% cut is a 5% cut. Most weeks the right answer is a short list, or no bets at all.
  • It can't out-think the market. We're not building a better ratings system than a dozen US books with full-time staff. We're finding where TAB's price is out of date. Smaller advantage, but a real one.
  • The middle US price isn't the last word. It's the best free reference there is. When the US books disagree with each other by more than a point, we trust it less, and passing is better than guessing.
  • We still haven't settled how to take the bookies' cut out. Two ways of doing it — splitting the cut evenly, or leaning it towards the favourite — move every number by up to 2.4 points. Until we agree which one and test it, don't read anything into small differences.
  • In Week 1 there's no form from this season at all, and most teams haven't filed an injury report when the prices go up. Week 1 is a numbers exercise, not a football read.
